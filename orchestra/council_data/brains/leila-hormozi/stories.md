# Stories: Leila Hormozi

Built from the eight batch notes over 48 video sources (2021 to 2026). 20 business and leadership stories she reuses, with the lesson she draws. Personal and family history is left out on purpose. Source ids in parentheses; where her numbers differ between retellings, the versions are listed.

1. **The boss who left, and the boss who replaced him.** At her first gym job her manager gave clear expectations, one-on-ones, predictable meetings, and encouragement. His replacement swore, threatened, and was unpredictable, and the team fell from the top of the region to mediocre in about three months while the best trainers left. Lesson: leaders create the environment, and people hate the boss, not the work. She calls it the reason she wanted to build a company: "it's really that instance that led me to want to build a company" (S035, S005, S003).

2. **The whiteboard split.** Early at Gym Launch a mentor wrote the business functions on a whiteboard, gave marketing to Alex and everything else to Leila, and told Alex not to help her. Lesson: clear lanes plus autonomy, and a senior person who helps too much keeps the other from believing in themselves: "This is Alex and then all the rest is Leila." (S008, S006).

3. **Motels to $50M a year.** She and Alex lived in motels and extended stays for about the first 16 months, trying webinars and a gym turnaround offer before one thing worked (S019). Revenue then ran fast, though her retellings differ: zero to "$2.5 million a month in like less than a year" (S027); 7M, 27M, 35M, 37M, about 30M in COVID, about 40M projected (S043, 2022); "zero to 7 million to 27 million to 36 million to 50 m" (S032, 2024); about $50M a year within four years (S029). Lesson: busy is not growth, and growth outran her skills.

4. **The missing talent funnel.** Gym Launch stalled near $400,000 a month because sales capacity could not keep up, and a top rep quit. Her mentor asked about her talent acquisition funnel; she copied the client funnel for hiring and went from $400,000 to $4 million a month in 20 months without changing the offer or the sales process. Her first sales team, college friends who all thought alike, could not scale it (S037). Lesson: "Talent acquisition is the missing funnel in your business" (S037).

5. **Delegated hiring, crumbling culture.** In the early years she handed hiring to managers and stopped taking last interviews, and watched the culture fall apart within about five months. After a record $4.5 million month she chose to rebuild the company she wanted to show up to rather than quit (S039). Around the same size she was in about 37 hours of meetings a week and realized the problem was her running them badly (S040). Lesson: "it all starts with the hiring process" (S039).

6. **The 2019 breaking point and the lost superstar.** From 2016 she hired anyone with a pulse; the team grew from seven to 21 and the people who pushed for more pay and time off were the ones who left (S042, S024). After 2019 she built a five-part employee value proposition. Of about four superstars at her last company, three had clear paths and stayed through the sale; she lost the fourth because "I just didn't have anywhere to move them to" (S024). Lesson: the vision must be big enough for top talent's own vision.

7. **Overhiring and the layoffs she owned.** Gym Launch once had about 15 people with nothing to do (S030). Prestige Labs hired 20 customer service reps for launch demand and needed six after three months; one rep said: "Leila I've taken one email and I've been here for 7 hours ethically I can't keep working here" (S032). She told the team the layoffs were her fault, overpaid severance, pre-wrote recommendation letters, and lined up interviews (S030, S041). Lesson: do not solve short-term pain with permanent hires.

8. **The Gym Launch sale, retold with different numbers.** The constant: buyers told them to wait a year and add technology, COVID hit, and the sale price fell far below the earlier valuation. The numbers move between tellings:
   - 2023-10: PE firms valued it at $150M about 19 months before the sale and said waiting could mean $350M to $500M; it sold for about $50M (S006).
   - 2024-11: "if you wait a year you might be able to get 350 400 million for it and then we wait a year and then we got 50 million" (S002), with a pre-COVID figure of about $250M.
   - 2024-12: she opens with a $46.2M sale price (S027).
   - 2026-06: "We were supposed to sell for $150 million. So, like, never felt like a success." (S010)
   Lessons she draws: sell when you do not need to, keep options, and outside forces can kill a plan. She also says "if I went back with the skills I have now I would have never sold the company" (S006). Treat any single number as approximate.

9. **The team that made the sale worth more.** Two years before the sale she led every department; she installed leaders until she was not needed. Buyers asked questions she could not answer because her team ran the company, and "the more that they got to know our team the more valuable they realized the business was" (S027). Her favorite moment was writing team members checks bigger than any money they had seen (S012). A married couple promoted from within replaced her and Alex as CEO and operator (S043).

10. **The software venture argument.** Alex wanted to build software; she thought it would cost millions. They argued for three days, agreed to shut it down if it was not profitable within four months, and it made a profit and sold (S008, 2022). Lesson: "if you don't agree, don't move forward" (S008), and when you must, set a kill date. Her 2026 account mentions two software products built over two years, one sold in a stock deal and one unused (S017); it is unclear whether these overlap.

11. **The food business she killed.** With Alex she sold nationwide meals, spending about $1.1M to $2M; it made little money and brought heavy support issues: "Not worth the juice for the squeeze." (S016). One account says they pulled the plug about six months in (S016), another that it flopped after a year (S017). Lesson: "The market doesn't care that we took a year." (S016)

12. **COVID at Gym Launch.** She cut to the bone and told the team she was pausing birthdays, anniversaries, and kudos for three to four months, and why (S031). With about 100 people asking whether they had a job tomorrow, she could not promise but could show certainty, and kept a "be neutral" post-it on her computer (S029, S020). Lesson: "I could let them borrow my sense of certainty until they had one for themselves" (S029).

13. **Firing two leaders, out loud.** Growing from about 30 to 75 people, she hired about 10 leaders in six months and fired two who treated people badly despite strong resumes, then told the team why. Other, less experienced leaders started repeating the decision: "It was like singularly one of the best decisions I've made because I got to watch all these other leaders who were less experienced repeat that." (S016). Lesson: a leader's decision models behavior.

14. **The $9 million dumb tax.** She and Alex unwound their three worst financial decisions and found about $9M of avoidable cost, about half from one wrong person in the wrong role for about a year: "i would have nine million more dollars in my bank if i had not made the three" (S044). In her worked example on an underperforming director of marketing, she chose to fire, run the department herself in the interim, and hire higher (S044). Lesson: avoid dumb decisions before chasing brilliant ones.

15. **Investing in the horse instead of the jockey.** Early Acquisition.com deals were made because the opportunity looked great: "we were so blinded by the opportunity being so good that we invest in it anyways" (S012). She divested a number of companies about a year and a half later and says she leaned on other people's rules over first principles (S007). One bad investment of close to $10M did not change her process (S014). She walked away from buying an agency whose 50-some clients turned out to be 6 (S027). Each quarter founders rate themselves; the ones who failed rated themselves higher than she did (S007). Lesson: "bet on the jockey not the horse" (S012).

16. **Retitling the job.** Changing "administrative assistant" to "senior administrative assistant" moved applicants from about one year of experience to five or six (S003). An analyst role open for 2.5 months filled in a week as "senior financial analyst"; "student happiness lead" filled in a week as "head of customer success" (S037). Lesson: "the job title is the equivalent to the headline" (S037).

17. **Promotion cases from her teams.** Sean, a top rep, hated being sales manager after three weeks because the reward was delayed; Mel took two weeks of daily coaching and still was not ready to manage; Lily trained others after her shift and became director of customer success for two companies; Maggie, a COO, doubled her feedback requests after treating her team as loyal followers; Kale thought short-term until he became the one giving the annual vision talk (S043). Kale later chose the CEO path from two she laid out and stayed three years after the sale (S028). Her EA Yasmine became director of operations by proximity (S028). Lesson: build the skill before the title, and "you have to see your team as the product that creates the other thing" (S043).

18. **Speed is king.** Gym Launch customer service answered in about 90 seconds on average, so she promised five minutes and set the internal target at two; customers were impressed when answers came in two. Value, promise, department KPI, and role KPI all flowed from one line (S034). Lesson: "values give you a framework to make decisions that have not occurred yet" (S034).

19. **Referrals and the eleven details.** At Gym Launch referrals fell from 45% of sales to 30%; running more ads pushed them to 20% and satisfaction from 90 to 70: "we have not gotten better we basically band-aided a wound that needed to be sew and shut" (S029). Years later, when workshop word of mouth dipped, she sat in three workshops and fixed about 11 details (greeters, lunch, goodbyes, names), and the stats came back within one workshop (S026). Lesson: "I beg of you try and fix 11 details before you try and to fix one big strategy" (S026).

20. **Acquisition.com 3.0 and the partner she brought in.** After doubling headcount in nine to ten months, she told the team their talent had been covering for missing process and that her own spread-thin style had to change first: "this version of Leila doesn't scale this company and I have to set the standard for how everybody else here operates." (S021). With every division rolling up to her, she brought in Chiron (captions also spell it Shiron) as the first partner; he took finance, tech, and legal (S023, S013), and in 2026 she moved to executive chairwoman and handed him the CEO role (S015). Lesson: the founder is often the one who has to change.
